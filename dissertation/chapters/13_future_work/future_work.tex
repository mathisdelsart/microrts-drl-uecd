\chapter{Future Work}
\label{chap:future-work}

This chapter outlines directions for future work, building on the limitations identified in Chapter~\ref{chap:limitations}. Section~\ref{sec:fw-short-term} proposes four short-term research directions that draw directly on the artifacts of Chapters~\ref{chap:python_bridge} to~\ref{chap:results}. Section~\ref{sec:fw-long-term} then steps back to a longer horizon, drawing on a cross-game research line on transferable representations and general game systems to outline a program that uses MicroRTS as a stepping stone toward general RTS AI.

\section{Short-Term Directions}
\label{sec:fw-short-term}

The four directions below each augment the artifacts of this thesis with one additional mechanism: an external language model, a hierarchical decomposition, a symbolic prior module, or an adversarial map generator. The common thread is tractability: each direction prototypes on top of the existing codebase rather than rebuilding it, and each closes at least one limitation named in Chapter~\ref{chap:limitations}, from the pure-RL scope to the topology-shift collapse.

\subsection{Hybrid DRL and LLM Agents}

The first direction pairs \code{UECD} with an LLM that handles strategic decisions, keeping \code{UECD} as the per-tick executor~\cite{cuiEmpoweringLLMsParameterized2025, maAdaptiveCommandRealTime2025}. Three concrete instantiations are immediate. \textbf{\emph{(i)}} An \emph{LLM planner} would emit a game plan from the initial map and unit table (rush, expand, or a specific hybrid Light/Ranged composition), which \code{UECD} then executes. \textbf{\emph{(ii)}} An \emph{LLM opponent modeler}~\cite{guoSuspicionAgentPlayingImperfect2023, mallampatiDynamicStrategyAdaptation2025} would read the first $N$ frames of play through the per-component statistics exposed by the environment stack of Chapter~\ref{chap:python_bridge}, classify the opponent's strategy in natural language, and condition \code{UECD} via an embedding~\cite{perezFiLMVisualReasoning2018}. \textbf{\emph{(iii)}} An \emph{LLM curriculum generator} would produce new training scenarios on demand, replacing the fixed scripted pool with a constantly refreshed distribution.

What makes this actionable now is the modular Java--Python bridge of Chapter~\ref{chap:python_bridge}: the structured observations, per-opponent statistics, and replay machinery are exactly the surface a language model needs. The fit with the community is direct as well: the $2026$ MicroRTS competition has pivoted to LLM-based agents, so a hybrid LLM-DRL stack turns the agent of this thesis into a reusable building block rather than a final destination. Voyager~\cite{wangVoyagerOpenEndedEmbodied2023} has shown in Minecraft that an LLM-driven agent can produce coherent long-horizon behavior in a complex game environment, suggesting that the approach extends to RTS. This direction also relaxes the pure-RL scope of Section~\ref{sec:lim-deliberate}: strategy classes naturally expressible as compact priors come from an external language model instead of being rediscovered from scratch by gradient descent.

\subsection{Hierarchical Policy Decomposition}

The second direction questions the assumption that a single network should decide, at every tick and from a global view, what every unit on the map does. A more faithful split mirrors how human players, and real organizations, operate. A \emph{strategic policy} runs on a slow tick, setting army composition and the macro objective (push left, defend the home base, harass the opponent's expansion), while a set of \emph{per-unit micro policies} executes that objective at every tick from local observation only. Each unit gets its own perception and tactical autonomy, as an employee retains autonomy over the means while the manager fixes the goal. Half of the substrate is already built: the \code{UECD} encoder produces per-cell features usable as per-unit observations, and the entity-attention block of Section~\ref{sec:axis-relational} already reasons over units rather than over a flat grid. The missing piece is a principled coupling between the two levels, for which FeUdal Networks~\cite{vezhnevetsFeUdalNetworksHierarchical2017} and the Options framework~\cite{suttonMDPsSemiMDPsFramework1999} provide concrete starting points~\cite{jiangHierarchicalReinforcementLearning2025}, with macro-action selection in StarCraft~\cite{xuMacroActionSelection2018} the closest RTS precedent. This addresses two limitations of Chapter~\ref{chap:limitations} at once. GridNet's per-cell action representation (Section~\ref{sec:lim-deliberate}) constrains the policy to operate at the single-unit level, leaving no room for multi-unit macros or temporally extended sub-goals. The proposed strategic-tick policy supplies precisely that missing abstraction layer. The same decomposition closes the topology-shift collapse of Section~\ref{sec:gen-probes}: a monolithic policy with a global view is structurally biased to overfit to the geometry of its training map, while per-unit policies with local observations generalize more naturally because their input is less correlated with the global topology.

\subsection{Neuro-Symbolic Integration}

The third direction couples \code{UECD} with a symbolic reasoning module: a small set of compact, human-readable rules induced from tournament replays, of the form ``if the opponent has produced more Heavy than Ranged in the last $60$ ticks, prioritize Light.'' The rules condition the policy either through additional input channels or through a soft gating mechanism on the action head, so the neural network keeps its differentiable expressiveness while the symbolic part contributes interpretability~\cite{baeInterpretableReinforcementLearning2022} and sample-efficient priors. The artifacts of this thesis make the direction immediately tractable: the tournament replay corpus of Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation-framework} provides the data source for rule induction, and the modular per-component reward function of Chapter~\ref{chap:python_bridge} offers a natural insertion point for rule-derived priors. The direction also belongs to a broader line of work on the formal description of game knowledge through ludemes~\cite{pietteericLudiiLudemicGeneral2020} and on the use of explicit knowledge to constrain neural learners~\cite{silvestriInjectingDomainKnowledge2021}. It addresses two limitations of Chapter~\ref{chap:limitations}: the vulnerability of pure end-to-end policies under distribution shift, which symbolic priors absorb; and the statistical uncertainty imposed by the limited training budget, which knowledge injection offsets.

\subsection{Adversarial Co-Evolution and PCG}

The fourth direction replaces the fixed map pool by a procedural content generator (PCG)~\cite{sorochanGeneratingRealTimeStrategy2022} that produces maps, starting conditions, and unit-cost variants targeted at the difficulty frontier of the current agent. The agent and the generator co-evolve, with the generator rewarded for finding configurations that are challenging but solvable, following the open-ended-evolution scheme of POET~\cite{wangPairedOpenEndedTrailblazer2019} and the regret-based environment design of PAIRED~\cite{dennisEmergentComplexityZeroshot2021}. What makes this tractable in the present codebase is that the padded-observation pipeline and the SPP critic of Chapters~\ref{chap:architectures} and~\ref{chap:training-system} already support variable-size maps without retraining: the only missing component is the generator itself. The limitation this direction targets is the most explicit of the four, the topology-shift collapse of Section~\ref{sec:gen-probes}. Instead of hoping that a manually curated map curriculum eventually covers enough geometry to generalize, the agent trains against an adversary whose explicit job is to find topologies it fails on, and robustness becomes part of the training objective rather than a hoped-for side effect.

\section{Long-Term Vision}
\label{sec:fw-long-term}

Section~\ref{sec:lim-deliberate} identified the choice of MicroRTS over a commercial RTS as the first deliberate scope limitation, leaving the transfer of \code{UECD} and its training pipeline to commercial-scale environments as an open question. Having shown that DRL can produce a competitive agent on this abstraction, this section reframes that limitation as an opportunity: ``what can be transferred from MicroRTS toward larger RTS games, and how?'' The four subsections below each adapt one tool from a cross-game research line on transferable representations and general game systems, addressing a specific gap that this thesis raised but could not close. Together they sketch a longer-term program.

\subsection{Spatial State-Action Features}

The first tool comes from~\cite{soemersSpatialStateactionFeatures2023}, which learns local spatial patterns around game actions and uses them as features that generalize across structurally different board games. These features sit deliberately between hand-crafted heuristics and end-to-end deep networks: simple enough to transfer across games with different boards and pieces, rich enough to capture meaningful tactical structure. The analog for RTS is direct, since the convolutional encoder of \code{UECD} already encodes spatial-action features implicitly: the composition of units in a $5\times 5$ window around a move, the presence of enemy units within an attack pattern, the proximity of friendly resources. Making these features explicit and transferable would yield an RTS counterpart to those board-game features. The gap this would close is the topology brittleness identified in Chapter~\ref{chap:limitations}: a representation that captures local tactical structure independently of the global map geometry is one route to RTS competence that survives a layout change, and the convolutional substrate of \code{UECD} is precisely what makes a formal extraction protocol tractable.

\subsection{Cross-Variant Policy and Value Transfer}
\label{sec:fw-long-term-transfer}

The second tool is the transfer protocol of~\cite{mellaGeneralTransferApproach}, which demonstrates zero-shot and fine-tuning transfer of fully convolutional policy-value networks across structurally different board games. The protocol relies on the semantic alignment provided by Ludii~\cite{pietteericLudiiLudemicGeneral2020}: the source and target games share enough descriptive structure for the same network to be reused or fine-tuned across them. \code{UECD} already satisfies the architectural half of the prerequisites: the encoder is fully convolutional, and the SPP critic is scale-invariant, so the same protocol applies in principle. The experiments this enables are concrete and progressively ambitious: pre-train on the open-map pool of Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation-framework} and fine-tune on the closed maps; pre-train on the standard unit table and fine-tune on variants where unit costs or hit points are perturbed; in the longer term, pre-train on MicroRTS and fine-tune on Wargus-style environments\footnote{Open-source RTS engine based on Stratagus, GitHub: \url{https://github.com/Wargus/wargus}.} with different unit semantics. This direction answers the BC-portability bottleneck of Chapter~\ref{chap:limitations}: instead of regenerating supervised data for every new map, the transfer protocol reuses what the policy already knows~\cite{zhuangComprehensiveSurveyTransfer2019}.

\subsection{Self-Play Distribution Shaping}

The third tool is the self-play distribution manipulation of~\cite{soemersManipulatingDistributionsExperience2020}, which shows that reweighting episodes by length and injecting exploratory policies in Expert Iteration produces faster and more diverse self-play training. The self-play stack of Chapter~\ref{chap:training-system}, built on PFSP-hard sampling over a checkpoint pool, collects a largely undirected mix of trajectories and offers no lever to densify the regions of the trajectory space that matter most for learning. Applying the same mechanism would reweight the pool by episode length, opponent diversity, or terminal-reward variance, spending more of the self-play budget where the gradient signal is most informative. This becomes essential when self-play must span multiple maps or game variants, which is exactly the regime that the previous two subsections target. It also offers a direct lever on the monotone-strategy attractor of Section~\ref{sec:lim-open}: reweighting episodes by opponent diversity disrupts the homogeneous self-play distribution that lets a Ranged-only composition dominate the gradient signal during fine-tuning.

\subsection{A General RTS Game System}

The fourth direction is a program rather than a single experiment. Ludii~\cite{pietteericLudiiLudemicGeneral2020} covers turn-based games through ludemes, declarative high-level concepts that make rules concise and shared semantics explicit, which enables the transfer protocol of Section~\ref{sec:fw-long-term-transfer}. No equivalent exists for RTS games: real-time semantics such as simultaneous actions, resource economies, and fog-of-war sit outside the current ludeme grammar. This is one reason MicroRTS, Wargus, and the various StarCraft research environments~\cite{synnaeveTorchCraftLibraryMachine2016, andersenDeepRTSGame2018} have remained isolated codebases rather than instances of a common formalism. The long-horizon goal is to define an RTS counterpart of Ludii: a declarative game-description language able to describe these environments and future variants in a single substrate, with shared evaluation infrastructure and transfer protocols. The deliverables of this thesis, namely a competitive agent on one specific RTS abstraction, a reproducible tournament framework, and an open-source training pipeline, supply one of the concrete instances such a system would need to support. Such a substrate would be positioned to resolve the limitations identified in Chapter~\ref{chap:limitations}: single-map specialization, topology brittleness, and replay-bound warmstart.
